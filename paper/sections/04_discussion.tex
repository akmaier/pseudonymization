\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Four recommendations follow, and one prediction does not survive.

\textbf{Prefer a large union to a consensus rule.} Agreement buys precision the release does not
need and pays in recall, and every leakage measure follows recall. \textbf{State the attacker's
knowledge with the number.} A corpus-internal prior is an upper bound, not a risk, and here the two
differ by the whole result. \textbf{Report the denominator you release on.} No corpus-level score
describes a document, a case or a language, and the three disagree on every corpus we measured.

\textbf{Choose the replacement form for utility, not for privacy.} Since the placeholder release is
a rewrite of the surrogate release, neither is safer against an adversary who knows the scheme:
telling the ranker so lifts it from 0.085 to 0.205 on unchanged text. Its real advantage is that token-level tasks
survive it and do not survive \texttt{[PERSON]}; at document level the placeholder is better.

The prediction that recall moves the two attacks in opposite directions is refuted. Better detection
was expected to starve context linkage while leaving a larger population for frequency alignment.
Instead alignment recovered nothing at any recall and both linkage attacks fell as recall rose: the
attacks do not trade off, and both are bounded by the identifiers the detector missed.

The limits are specific. Linkage is measurable only on Enron and CARDIO:DE, because TAB and
OntoNotes link mentions within a document; people recur there, so those figures understate rather
than bound the risk. CARDIO:DE names were inserted into an already de-identified release, making its
frequency distribution synthetic and its clinical realism partial. Dates pass through by design, a
channel this pipeline does not close. The candidate-ranking attack presents ten names, bounding an
adversary with a shortlist rather than one searching a population. Every number describes one
release under one key.
