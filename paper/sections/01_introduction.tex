\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Clinical data are multimodal. A chest radiograph travels with its report, and a cardiology study
with its discharge letter. Anonymising pixels and prose are different open problems: the image may
require defacing or removal of burned-in text, while the report requires us to find and replace
identifiers in running language. Solving either alone does not permit sharing. Hence, a database is
releasable only when both halves are protected.

Artificial intelligence makes both tasks tractable at database scale. Yet, it also makes their
guarantees harder to state. Learned systems fail according to the distribution on which they are
used, and their errors need not be uniform across languages, scripts, document types, or identifier
classes. Packh\"auser et al.~\cite{packhauser2022reid} demonstrated the imaging version of this problem by
re-identifying patients from de-identified chest radiographs. Here, we measure its text analogue: whether identity
can still be recovered after the explicit identifiers have been transformed.

We use \emph{de-identification} as the broad process of reducing identifying information.
\emph{Pseudonymisation} is a reversible or linkable form of that process: an identifier is replaced
by a \emph{surrogate}, i.e. a realistic substitute such as another name. \emph{Anonymisation} aims
to prevent a person from being singled out or linked at all. This distinction matters because a
stable pseudonym preserves longitudinal utility, but it also gives an adversary a handle that
persists across records. European guidance makes this trade-off explicit and regards keyed message
authentication codes as a robust pseudonymisation technique~\cite{enisa2021pseudonymisation}.

Existing evaluation is split. Detection benchmarks report whether an identifying
\emph{span}---a contiguous sequence of tokens---was found. Utility studies report the change in a
downstream task. Re-identification studies report whether an attacker succeeds. These quantities
are rarely measured together, although evaluation frameworks argue that
technical performance, information loss, and an intruder test belong together
~\cite{mozes2021nointruder}. Surrogate systems and masking benchmarks study important pieces
~\cite{eder2019emails,pilan2022tab,osborne2022bratsynthetic}, but not all three costs in one design. We
therefore cannot say what a protection policy buys or costs.

Aggregate corpus scores create a second gap. A data-protection officer does not release an average
token. They release documents and cases concerning people. Note that one unmodified mention can
identify a person even when thousands of other identifier tokens were removed. We must therefore
ask whether any finding survives in each document, whether any case remains exposed, and whether
each entity remains linkable. An \emph{entity} is the underlying person, place, or organisation; a
\emph{co-reference chain} is the set of textual mentions that refer to that same entity.

Here, we evaluate one design on four corpora, covering clinical letters, legal judgments, news and
other genres, and e-mail in German, English, Chinese, and Arabic. Each document passes through the
same detector comparison, the same three release conditions, and the same measurement families.
This permits two tests. First, does detector precision govern utility more strongly than the choice
between surrogates and placeholders? Second, does improved recall move re-identification attacks in
opposite directions? Removing names may obstruct context linkage while creating a larger, stable
population for frequency matching. We connect detection, utility, exposure, attacks, and stability
on the same records.
